01 · DEFINITIONS

Invention, innovation, diffusion — three different events

Most confusion about "measuring invention" starts with using one word for three things. The measurement literature separates them precisely.

Invention is the creation of a working novelty — a device, process, or method that did not exist before. Innovation is that novelty implemented: the OECD's Oslo Manual, the international standard for innovation statistics, defines an innovation as "a new or improved product or process (or combination thereof) that differs significantly from the unit's previous products or processes and that has been made available to potential users… or brought into use," and states the boundary explicitly: "The requirement for implementation differentiates innovation from other concepts such as invention."TRUTH [1] Diffusion is the spread of an implemented innovation through a population of adopters — Everett Rogers' territory, with its S-curve and adopter categories (innovators 2.5%, early adopters 13.5%, early majority 34%, late majority 34%, laggards 16%).[2]

The separation is old. Schumpeter built his theory of development on the "carrying out of new combinations" (1911; English ed. 1934)[3] and in Business Cycles (1939) insisted that invention and innovation are distinct events — innovation can occur without new invention, and invention need not induce innovation (paraphrased; the standard citation is vol. 1, p. 84).[4] The activity that produces inventions is itself standardized for measurement by the OECD's Frascati Manual (first edition 1963; current 2015): R&D is "creative and systematic work undertaken in order to increase the stock of knowledge… and to devise new applications of available knowledge," and must be novel, creative, uncertain, systematic, and transferable and/or reproducible.[5]

The law's definitions came first, and they encode the same idea: Venice's 1474 statute protected "any new and ingenious device, not previously made"[7]; England's 1624 Statute of Monopolies reserved 14-year patents to "the true and first inventor" of "new manufactures"[8]; the US Constitution empowered Congress "to promote the Progress of Science and useful Arts" (1787)[9], implemented by the Patent Act of 1790.[10] Novelty, utility, and disclosure have been the legal skeleton of "invention" for five and a half centuries.

02 · THE ELEMENTS

What invention is made of

Ten load-bearing elements, each carried by the historical record rather than by anecdote. Together they are the parts list of the machinery.

Strip away the mythology and the record shows invention behaving like a process with recurring parts: a stock of prior art, mechanisms that recombine it, institutions that store, steer, and reward the work, and selection environments that decide what survives. The ten elements below recur from the 15th-century Venetian arsenal to the 21st-century research lab.

E1 · RECOMBINATION

New things are combinations of old things

Brian Arthur's structural analysis: "Technologies somehow must come into being as fresh combinations of what already exists" — components of technologies are themselves technologies. Schumpeter's development was already the "carrying out of new combinations." Invention grows out of the existing stock, which is why it compounds.

Arthur 2009; Schumpeter 1911/1934 [11][3]
E2 · CUMULATION

Knowledge stacks; nobody starts from zero

Newton to Hooke, Feb 1675/6: "If I have seen further it is by standing on the sholders of Giants" — itself a 12th-century metaphor (Bernard of Chartres) that Newton was reusing. Every inventor inherits the full prior stack; the stack only grows.

Newton letter, Historical Society of Pennsylvania [12]
E3 · INEVITABILITY

Ripe inventions arrive in multiples

Ogburn & Thomas catalogued 148 inventions made independently by two or more people; Merton argued multiples are the norm and singletons the anomaly. Bell's telephone application and Gray's caveat reached the Patent Office the same day — Feb 14, 1876. Darwin and Wallace were read jointly on Jul 1, 1858. When the prerequisites exist, someone assembles them.

PSQ 1922; Merton 1961; patent records [13][14][15][16]
E4 · THE ADJACENT POSSIBLE

Only the next step is reachable

Kauffman's term (1996/2000): at any moment, the space of buildable things is bounded by what exists now; each invention opens new doors. It explains both multiples (many people stand at the same door) and why "ahead of its time" usually means missing complements — calculus needed 17th-century mathematics, not more genius, to be found twice.

Kauffman 1996/2000; Johnson 2010; calculus priority record [17][18][19]
E5 · STEERING BY DEMAND & PRICES

Markets point the search

Schmookler: capital-goods patenting tracked investment demand (railroads) — invention follows where buyers are (later re-estimation weakened, but did not erase, the pattern). Hicks: a change in relative factor prices "is itself a spur to invention… directed to economizing the use of a factor which has become relatively expensive."

Schmookler 1966 (+ critique); Hicks 1932 [20][21]
E6 · INSTITUTIONS

Rules that store, reward, and disclose

Venice 1474, the Statute of Monopolies 1624, the US Patent Act 1790: property in ideas, in exchange for disclosure. The Royal Society (1660) institutionalized open verification. Mokyr's "Republic of Letters" thesis — a competitive market for ideas as the origin of sustained growth — earned half of the 2025 economics Nobel, awarded "for having explained innovation-driven economic growth." Caveat from the record: Moser's world's-fair evidence shows patent laws shaping the direction of invention more clearly than its level.

Statutes; Royal Society; Mokyr 2016; Nobel 2025; Moser 2005/2013 [7][8][10][22][23][24][25]
E7 · ORGANIZATION

Invention industrialized itself

Edison's Menlo Park (1876) promised "a minor invention every ten days and a big thing every six months or so." GE built the first US industrial lab (1900); Bell Labs consolidated at ~3,600 staff (1925). Individual inventors out-patented corporations until 1933; today ~86% of US-origin utility patents go to businesses. The unit of invention moved from person to system.

Rutgers Edison Papers; IEEE Milestones; Smithsonian/NSF patent-share data; Lamoreaux & Sokoloff [26][27][28][29][30]
E8 · PREPARED SERENDIPITY

Accidents reward the trained observer

Pasteur, Douai, Dec 7, 1854: "chance favors only the prepared mind." Fleming's contaminated plate (Sep 3, 1928), Goodyear's hot stove (1839; patent 3,633, 1844), Spencer's melted chocolate bar at a magnetron (1945), Röntgen's glowing screen (Nov 8, 1895): each accident happened in front of someone equipped to recognize it — and inside an institution able to develop it.

Wikisource 1854; ACS; USPTO; IEEE Spectrum; APS [31][32][33][34][35][36]
E9 · VARIATION & SELECTION

Technology evolves; markets select

Basalla: continuity, diversity, novelty, selection — an evolutionary account of artifacts. Nelson & Winter: firms carry routines like genes; innovation is search; markets are the selection environment. Invention supplies variation; selection decides what becomes innovation.

Basalla 1988; Nelson & Winter 1982 [37][38]
E10 · OPEN EXCHANGE

Sharing accelerates the whole district

Allen's "collective invention": Cleveland-district (England) ironmasters freely revealed furnace designs, and the whole industry ratcheted forward for ~20 years. The Royal Society's journals, Mokyr's Republic of Letters, and modern open source are the same mechanism at different scales: disclosure compounds the stock everyone recombines from (E1, E2).

Allen 1983; Royal Society; Mokyr 2016 [39][22][23]
verdict · the lone-genius model of invention

[TRUTH] Multiples are the documented norm — 148 catalogued cases by 1922; same-day telephone filings; joint Darwin–Wallace reading. [13][15][16]

[TRUTH] The incandescent lamp had "nearly a dozen" active inventors in the 1870s before Edison's carbon-filament system won (Friedel & Israel; US DOE). [40]

[TRUTH] Team share of knowledge production has risen for 70 years; mean S&E team size grew from under 2 to ~3.5 authors by 2000. [41]

[FALSITY] "Great inventions are the work of solitary geniuses." Lemley's review of the legal-historical record: nearly all significant inventions were made near-simultaneously by multiple teams. [42]

=> The lone-genius account: FALSITY — individuals matter, but as the fastest assemblers of a ripe adjacent possible, not as sole causes.

03 · THE PROCESS

A century of process models, converging

The models of how invention happens were themselves invented, tested, and revised. Read in sequence, they converge on one architecture: staged, feedback-ridden, and selective.

Every serious process model since 1926 has kept stages and added feedback. Wallas named the cognitive stages; Usher embedded them in cumulative history; the postwar "linear model" (basic research → applied → product) turned out to be a retrospective construction that its supposed author never drew; Kline and Rosenberg replaced it with chains and loops; Cooper turned the loops into managed gates; Altshuller showed the patterns could be mined from the patent record itself.

1926

Wallas — the four stages

Preparation → Incubation → Illumination → Verification (with an "Intimation" sub-stage, per later re-reading). The cognitive core every later model keeps.[43]

1929 / 1954

Usher — cumulative synthesis

Perception of the problem → setting the stage → act of insight → critical revision. Insight is real but bracketed by preparation and by the long grind afterwards.[44]

JUL 1945

Bush — Science, the Endless Frontier

Basic research as "the pacemaker of technological progress"; the report that built the NSF — and got retrofitted into a "linear model" of innovation.[45]

1946

Altshuller — TRIZ

Invention patterns mined from ~40,000 patent abstracts: 40 inventive principles, contradiction matrix. The first systematic demonstration that the inventive step itself has regularities. (Larger patent counts claimed by the TRIZ community are undocumented.)[46]

1943 / 1962

Ryan & Gross → Rogers — diffusion

The Iowa hybrid-corn study (257 farmers, near-total adoption in ~13 years) founded diffusion research; Rogers systematized the S-curve and adopter categories.[47][2]

1986

Kline & Rosenberg — the chain-linked model

"Models that depict innovation as a smooth, well-behaved linear process badly misspecify the nature and direction of the causal factors at work." Research is called on all along the chain; use feeds back into design.[6]

1986 / 1990

Cooper — Stage-Gate

The funnel operationalized: staged development with Go/Kill gates — selection built into the process rather than left to the market's end.[48]

2006

Godin — the linear model debunked

Historiography: the "linear model" was assembled retrospectively in three steps by different communities. The tidy pipeline was never anyone's empirical finding.[49]

Basis discipline — the same world, three totals

  • China vs US: in PPP terms China's R&D ($1.028T) edged past the US ($1.009T) in 2024[51]; at market exchange rates China remains roughly half the US level (OECD, March 2026 release).[60] Which "truth" you get depends on the conversion you choose.
  • Patents-in-force series broke between WIPI editions (~17M-range in the 2024 edition vs 19.7M in 2025) due to coverage revisions — compare only within one edition.[52]

Input metrics

The workhorse input KPIs are GERD (gross domestic expenditure on R&D), R&D intensity (GERD/GDP), researchers per million, and risk capital. The US spent $937B in 2023 (est. $993B in 2024) — split $138B basic research, $174B applied, $625B experimental development[61] — with business funding 75% and the federal government 18%, an exact inversion of 1964 (business 31%, federal 67%).[62] China reported ¥3.61T for 2024 (+8.3%, intensity 2.68%) and ¥3.93T for 2025 (intensity 2.8%).[63] Government R&D budgets across the OECD fell 4.1% in real terms in 2024, with defence the main rising category.[60]

Output metrics

Patents are the oldest continuous output series — the US register alone runs from Samuel Hopkins' potash patent (July 31, 1790) to ~12.7 million numbered patents by August 2026.[53] The 2024 world totals: 3.7M applications (+4.9%), ~2.1M grants, 19.7M in force; 11.7M trademark applications; 1.22M industrial-design applications.[52] The PCT route — one international application — ran 275,900 filings in 2025, led by China (26.7%) and the US (19.1%).[65]

Outcome metrics — where invention meets the economy

The outcome layer is productivity. Solow's 1957 decomposition attributed 87.5% of the 1909–1949 doubling of US output per hour to technical change[66] — the founding demonstration that invention, not capital accumulation, drives long-run growth. The modern readings are sobering: US TFP growth ran ~1.98%/yr in 1948–1972 but only ~0.87%/yr across 1973–2019, and post-2004 sits below even the 1973–96 slowdown[67][68]; Gordon puts post-1970 TFP growth at roughly one-third of the 1920–1970 rate.[69]

A natural experiment in metric behavior: pharma

Drug R&D is the best-instrumented invention pipeline on earth, and it shows both metric pathologies and real signal. Eroom's Law (Moore's, backwards): approved drugs per inflation-adjusted billion dollars of R&D halved roughly every 9 years from 1950 to 2010 — an ~80-fold efficiency decline[70] — before breaking upward around 2010.[71] Cost per approved drug: $2.558B pre-approval (Tufts, 2013 dollars)[72]; Deloitte's cohort average ran $2.23B with 5.9% IRR in 2024, improving to $2.67B with 7.0% IRR in 2025.[73]

Firm-level KPIs — the practitioner's panel

Corporate R&D concentrates hard: the world's top 2,000 corporate investors spent €1,446B in 2024 — over 90% of all business-funded R&D — with US firms carrying 47.1% of it; Amazon entered the ranking at #1 (~€65B, a Scoreboard estimate), and FY2024 filings show Alphabet at $49.3B and Meta at $43.9B.[75][76] The standard firm KPI panel:

  • R&D intensity (R&D / net sales): pharma & biotech lead at ~16%, software & computer services ~12% (EU Scoreboard sector data; edition vintages vary).IGNORANCE — current-edition sector split not verified; treat the levels as indicative. [75]
  • New Product Vitality Index (3M, 1988): share of revenue from products launched in the past five years; 3M's benchmark ~30%.[77]
  • RQ (Research Quotient): Knott's estimated output elasticity of R&D — the closest thing to a firm-level "return on invention."[78]
  • Funnel metrics: stage-gate pass rates, time-to-market (no standardized definition — start and end points vary by firm), patents per R&D dollar.[48]
  • Composite indices: the Global Innovation Index 2025 (139 economies) ranks Switzerland #1 for the 15th consecutive year; China entered the top 10 for the first time. GII 2026 is due Sep 29, 2026 — after this paper.[79]
verdict · can invention be measured?

[TRUTH] Inputs and formal outputs are measured to international standard (Frascati 2015; Oslo 2018; WIPO series back to the 19th century). [5][1][52]

[TRUTH] Output metrics are gameable and propensity-confounded: firms rank secrecy and lead time above patents as protection (1,478-lab survey); China's filing subsidies produced documented volume-without-quality; "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." [80][81]

[FALSITY] "Patent counts measure invention rates." They measure filings: ~5% of US patents are licensed for royalty, ~1.5% litigated, "perhaps 95%" never valuable enough for either (Lemley, ca. 2001). [82]

[IGNORANCE] The current patent→value conversion rate.DATA REQUEST: a post-2001 replication of Lemley's licensing/litigation rates on modern USPTO cohorts.

=> Invention is measurable — as a battery of imperfect gauges (inputs + outputs + outcomes), never as any single number.

05 · THE LESSONS

Thirteen things history actually established

Each lesson is stated as a claim, with its evidentiary basis and epistemic state. These thirteen are the premises the trajectory section is allowed to extrapolate from.

Invention is recombination, so it compounds TRUTH

New technologies are combinations of existing ones; each addition enlarges the space of possible next combinations. This is why the process accelerates across centuries rather than exhausting itself.

Arthur 2009; Schumpeter 1911/1934; Kauffman's adjacent possible [11][3][17]

Ripe inventions arrive on schedule, in multiples TRUTH

148 documented independent co-inventions by 1922; same-day telephone filings (Feb 14, 1876); Darwin–Wallace read jointly (1858). When prerequisites and demand are present, the invention is close to inevitable — the interesting variable is who assembles it first, not whether.

Ogburn & Thomas 1922; Merton 1961; Lemley 2012 [13][14][42]

The lone genius is bad history FALSITY of the myth

The canonical solo inventors dissolve on inspection ("nearly a dozen" incandescent-lamp inventors in the 1870s), and knowledge work has shifted decisively to teams: mean S&E team size grew from under 2 to ~3.5 authors in 45 years, and teams produce the most-cited work.

Lemley 2012; US DOE lamp history; Wuchty, Jones & Uzzi 2007 [42][40][41]

Institutions are the operating system TRUTH

Patents (1474 → 1624 → 1790) bought disclosure with temporary exclusivity; open-verification societies (1660) made knowledge cumulative and trustworthy; the 2025 economics Nobel was awarded precisely "for having explained innovation-driven economic growth" through institutions and creative destruction. Nuance: patent laws demonstrably steer invention's direction; their effect on its level is contested.

Venice 1474; Statute of Monopolies; Royal Society; Nobel 2025; Moser 2005/2013 [7][8][22][24][25]

Invention industrialized: person → lab → team → system TRUTH

Menlo Park scheduled invention ("a minor invention every ten days"); GE (1900) and Bell Labs (1925) made it a corporate function; individual inventors out-patented firms until 1933, while ~86% of US-origin utility patents now go to businesses. The production function of invention is an organizational variable, not a constant.

Rutgers Edison Papers; IEEE Milestones; Smithsonian/NSF shares; Lamoreaux & Sokoloff [26][27][28][29][30]

Demand and relative prices steer the search TRUTH — direction; magnitude debated

Railroad patenting tracked railroad investment (Schmookler); dear factors invite economizing inventions (Hicks 1932). Re-estimation weakened Schmookler's correlations, so the lesson is directional steering, not demand-determinism.

Schmookler 1966 + Research Policy critique; Hicks 1932 [20][21]

Impact arrives decades after invention TRUTH

Electric motors were <5% of US factory drive power in 1899 — 17 years after Edison's first central station — and passed 50% only in the early 1920s, when the productivity surge finally appeared. Solow's 1987 quip ("the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics") marked the same lag for IT; the J-curve formalizes it via intangible complements.

David 1990; Solow 1987; Brynjolfsson, Rock & Syverson 2021 [83][84][85]

Science and invention have fused TRUTH

Patents citing science rose from 6.7% (1976) to 25.6% (2018); 61% of US patents trace back to a research article and 80% of cited articles link forward to a patent. Meanwhile large firms retreated from doing science themselves (publishing firms 17%→6%, 1980–2006) — buying the goose's eggs while releasing the goose.

Marx & Fuegi 2020; Ahmadpoor & Jones 2017; Arora, Belenzon & Patacconi 2018 [86][87][88]

Each idea costs more than the last TRUTH — with a live dissent

Aggregate US research productivity fell ×41 since the 1930s (≈−5.1%/yr); a Moore's-law doubling now takes >18× the researchers of the early 1970s; age at first invention rose ~0.6 years/decade as the knowledge burden grew; drug R&D efficiency halved every ~9 years for six decades before breaking upward around 2010. Dissent exists (Census WP 25-29: patents per R&D input rising) and is flagged in §06.

Bloom et al. 2020; Jones 2009; Scannell 2012; Ringel 2020; Census 2025 [89][90][70][71][91]

Growth held because effort scaled TRUTH

The response to falling research productivity was more researchers and more money: global R&D reached $3.48T (2024), and in PPP terms China's spend passed the US in 2024 — the first change at the top since the series began. Scaling inputs is the historical answer to declining idea-yield.

Bloom et al. 2020 (effort doubling); NSF 2026 [89][51]

Cost curves are the most predictable regularity we have TRUTH

Wright's 1936 aircraft learning curve generalizes: across 50+ technologies, cost declines follow statistically forecastable exponentials. Solar modules fell $106/W (1976) → ~$0.38/W (~99.6%), at a 20.2% learning rate per doubling of cumulative capacity. Where a deployment-driven curve exists, forecasting invention's effects becomes tractable even when individual inventions stay unpredictable.

Wright 1936; Farmer & Lafond 2016; Way et al. 2022; OWID [92][93][94][95]

Serendipity is a harvest, not a lottery TRUTH

Penicillin, vulcanization, X-rays, the microwave: each "accident" landed on a trained observer inside an institution able to develop it. Pasteur said it in 1854; the 20th-century lab systematized it. Slack time and exploratory capacity are inputs, not waste.

Pasteur 1854; Fleming 1928; Röntgen 1895; Spencer 1945; 3M Post-it [31][32][35][34][36]

Openness accelerates the whole district TRUTH

Cleveland's ironmasters ratcheted an entire industry by publishing furnace designs; the Republic of Letters did it for Enlightenment Europe; patents themselves are a disclosure bargain. Sharing enlarges the common stock that everyone recombines from — the compounding term in Lesson 1.

Allen 1983; Mokyr 2016; Royal Society [39][23][22]

Estimating forward without inventing the future

This section extrapolates only the regularities established in §05, reports published estimate ranges where experts disagree, and names the data that would falsify each projection. It is an extrapolation, not a prophecy.

6.1 · The scissors: more effort required, fewer hands available

Two measured curves are closing on each other. The first is research productivity: holding growth constant has required doubling research effort every ~13 years.[89] The second is demography: the researcher pipeline ultimately draws on population, and the UN projects world population to peak around 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s — it has already peaked in 63 countries.[96] Chad Jones' growth theory makes the collision explicit: with sub-replacement fertility, idea-based growth models converge to an "Empty Planet" outcome of stagnating living standards.[97]

Forecast humility — the base rate of confident prediction

  • FALSITY Watson's 1943 "world market for maybe five computers" — apocryphal; no primary evidence exists (it grew from a misread 1953 remark about one machine).[129] The most-quoted forecasting failure in tech history never happened.

07 · METHOD & LIMITS

How this paper knows what it claims

The apparatus, disclosed. What was verified, how, and where the edges of confidence sit.

Method

This paper was researched on 2026-09-03 by three parallel deep-research passes — (a) the history and theory of invention, (b) measurement systems and current KPI values, (c) evidence bearing on the future — each instructed to verify every claim against primary or authoritative sources (WIPO, OECD, NSF NCSES, UN DESA, journal and publisher pages, the Nobel Foundation, Our World in Data) and to mark anything unverifiable as unusable. The assembled draft was then adversarially red-teamed claim-by-claim before release: citations spot-checked, numbers re-derived where possible, attributions tested against quote-attribution research.

Three writing rules bind every section. Numbers carry citations — a figure with no bracketed source does not appear. Claims carry states — TRUTH (verified against the cited source), FALSITY (a documented myth or overclaim), IGNORANCE (open, with an explicit request for the data that would settle it). The future section extrapolates; it does not prophesy — it projects only regularities that have already survived measurement, and where published expert estimates disagree, it reports the disagreement as a range rather than resolving it by preference.

Limitations — read before citing this paper onward

  • Survivorship bias is structural. The history of invention is written from inventions that worked; the denominator of failed attempts is mostly unrecorded, so success-factor claims are weaker than they look.
  • Formal metrics undercount informal invention. Patents and R&D budgets measure institutionalized, patentable invention; tacit know-how, process tweaks, and open-source contribution are systematically underweighted.
  • Patent counts confound output with propensity. Differences across countries, industries, and eras partly reflect willingness and incentive to patent, not inventive activity itself.
  • Data lag. Official global statistics run one to three years behind; "latest" figures below are the latest published, not the current instant.
  • The forecast is conditional. Section 06 is an extrapolation of measured trends plus published estimate ranges. Confidence decays with horizon; every stated projection names its condition. Nothing in it is a prediction of specific events.
  • Source-access bias. English-language and open-access sources are overrepresented; some primary datasets sit behind paywalls and were verified through their publishers' own summaries.
